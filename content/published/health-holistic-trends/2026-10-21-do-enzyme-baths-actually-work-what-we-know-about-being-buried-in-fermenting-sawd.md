Enzyme baths are warm, and that part is real. Whether the enzymes do anything for your body is another matter: no solid clinical evidence shows they do. The experience is better understood as a form of heat therapy with a fermentation twist.

## What is an enzyme bath?

An enzyme bath is a spa treatment in which a person lies in a bed of plant material, such as rice bran or cypress sawdust, and is covered so that only the face is exposed. The mixture is fermented, and the microbial activity gives off heat. The treatment also exists in Japan, where it is called *kōso-buro* ([Jisho](https://jisho.org/search/酵素風呂)), and [The Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10905282) has reported on it as Korea's latest wellness trend. The Herald says temperatures reach 60 to 70°C, with buried sessions lasting about 20 to 30 minutes.

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction. In fermentation, microbes and enzymes break down the plant material, and that breakdown releases heat. The bath is warm because of this process.

## What do enzyme baths claim to do?

Spas and promoters tend to describe the baths as detoxifying, circulation-boosting and good for skin and stress ([Osmosis Day Spa](https://osmosis.com/cedar-enzyme-bath/benefits/); [The Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10905282)). These are marketing claims, and they vary by venue.

## What does the evidence show?

For the enzyme-specific claims, very little. The term "detox" has no clear meaning here. The liver and kidneys handle waste removal, and we found no study showing that sweating in a fermenting bath adds to that. A [PubMed search](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=%22enzyme%20bath%22%5Btiab%5D%20OR%20%22enzyme%20baths%22%5Btiab%5D%20OR%20%22koso%20buro%22%5Btiab%5D%20OR%20%22kouso%20buro%22%5Btiab%5D%20OR%20%22fermented%20sawdust%20bath%22%5Btiab%5D%20OR%20%22rice%20bran%20bath%22%5Btiab%5D%20OR%20%22rice%20bran%20baths%22%5Btiab%5D) for those terms in study titles and abstracts, run in October 2026, returned no studies at all, so no clinical trials of enzyme baths.

The heat is a different story. Passive heat exposure has been studied, mostly in saunas. A [2015 study](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.8187) in *JAMA Internal Medicine* by Laukkanen and colleagues followed 2,315 middle-aged Finnish men for a median of about 20 years and found that more frequent sauna use was associated with lower rates of fatal cardiovascular events. The study was observational, which means it can show a link but not prove that saunas caused the difference. It also looked at traditional Finnish saunas, which researchers studying the same group of men describe as having air with a relative humidity of 10 to 20% ([Kunutsor and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9792415/)), not buried-in-sawdust baths.

Related evidence comes from balneotherapy, the medical term for bathing in mineral water or mud. A [2007 Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD006864_balneotherapy-osteoarthritis) of seven trials (498 patients) with osteoarthritis found that mineral baths may ease pain compared with no treatment, but the authors called the evidence weak because of the poor quality of the trials. A [2015 Cochrane review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD000518_balneotherapy-or-spa-therapy-rheumatoid-arthritis) of nine studies (579 people) with rheumatoid arthritis found the evidence insufficient to show that balneotherapy works better than no treatment, and rated the quality of the evidence very low. Either way, this research speaks to warm-water or mud soaking in general, not to enzyme baths.

## Why does it feel good, then?

Heat raises heart rate and sends more blood to the skin ([Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/saunas-and-your-health)). Lying still in a quiet room for 20 to 30 minutes, the session length the Korea Herald reports, also has its own appeal. Neither needs enzymes to explain it, and we found no study showing that the fermentation adds anything beyond warmth.

## Are there risks?

Yes, and they come mostly from the heat. Sauna heat can cause dehydration and low blood pressure, and Poison Control advises leaving right away if you feel dizzy, lightheaded or sick ([Poison Control](https://www.poison.org/articles/are-saunas-good-for-you)). On top of that, buried bodies can't easily cool off or move. People with heart disease or uncontrolled high blood pressure are advised to check with a doctor before using a sauna ([Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/saunas-and-your-health)). People with low blood pressure are told to be cautious, and pregnant women are generally advised to avoid saunas ([Poison Control](https://www.poison.org/articles/are-saunas-good-for-you)). Heat can increase how much medicine passes into the body from skin patches, and Poison Control says people using nicotine or nitroglycerin patches should avoid saunas ([Poison Control](https://www.poison.org/articles/are-saunas-good-for-you)). Harvard Health advises avoiding medications that may impair sweating before and after a sauna ([Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/heart-health/saunas-and-your-health)). If you take any medication, check with a prescriber first.

One other point: because the material is a living fermenting mix, hygiene practices and how often it is replaced matter.

## What's the fair summary?

The trend rests on a pleasant heat experience wrapped in enzyme language that the science hasn't backed up. For a healthy adult, a short supervised session is probably a lot like a sauna. For people in the groups named above, it carries real risks. It is not a substitute for any prescribed treatment.

## A next step

If you're thinking of booking a session, ask the venue for the bath temperature and recommended session length, and run both past your doctor first.
