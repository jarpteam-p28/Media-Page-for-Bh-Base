Not automatically. A cheaper city only stretches your money if your income holds up and the things you value come with it. Rankings that pair cost with quality of life are a useful first filter, but they average out the details that decide whether a move helps a household.

Visual Capitalist recently published a chart comparing quality of life against cost across 99 cities in the Americas ([Visual Capitalist](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/cp/quality-of-life-vs-cost-in-99-cities-across-the-americas/)). It draws on Numbeo data, and both axes are percentile rankings within the sample. Charts like this follow a common pattern, and the pattern is worth understanding before it shapes a decision about where to live.

## What do cost-versus-quality rankings actually measure?

They combine two scores that come from very different kinds of data. Cost is usually built from prices for a basket of goods and services, such as groceries, transport, and restaurant meals. Some cost measures include rent and some do not: Numbeo's headline Cost of Living Index, for example, excludes rent and mortgage payments ([Numbeo](https://www.numbeo.com/cost-of-living/cpi_explained.jsp)). Quality of life is usually built from indexes covering things like safety, health care, pollution, commute times, and climate.

Some of these inputs are crowdsourced. Numbeo, a cost and quality database, combines crowdsourced reports with automated market-data collection ([Numbeo](https://www.numbeo.com/cost-of-living/)), which makes it broad but uneven. Visual Capitalist's chart used Numbeo and notes that the data reflects contributors' experiences rather than a representative survey ([Visual Capitalist](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/cp/quality-of-life-vs-cost-in-99-cities-across-the-americas/)). The result is a good snapshot of perception and rough prices. It is not a measurement of what a particular family will spend.

## Does a lower cost of living mean a lower quality of life?

Not as a rule. In Visual Capitalist's chart, cities appear in all four quadrants: some pair low costs with high quality of life, while New York, Los Angeles, and Chicago fall in the high-cost, low-quality corner ([Visual Capitalist](https://www.visualcapitalist.com/cp/quality-of-life-vs-cost-in-99-cities-across-the-americas/)). A cheaper city can score well on some measures and lower on others.

The useful reading of such a chart is the outliers: places where quality is high relative to cost, and places where prices are steep for what the indexes say you get. Those are worth a closer look. They are not proof of anything on their own.

## Why does housing explain so much of the gap?

Housing is the largest piece of the average household budget, at 33.4 percent of spending in 2024 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/housing-and-transportation-accounted-for-50-percent-of-household-spending-in-2024.htm)), and rents vary widely from place to place: in 2024, BEA's price index for housing rents ranged from 54.2 in West Virginia to 154.3 in California ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/real-personal-consumption-expenditures-state-and-real-personal-income-state-2024)). The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes Regional Price Parities, which express the price level in each state and metro area as a percentage of the overall national price level ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/data/prices-inflation/regional-price-parities-state-and-metro-area)). BEA's release on 2024 data says housing rents are often the main driver in differences in RPPs ([BEA](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/real-personal-consumption-expenditures-state-and-real-personal-income-state-2024)).

That matters because housing is a cost that is hard to trim by shopping around. Groceries and gas also differ from place to place, but a rent gap of several hundred dollars a month can outweigh those differences.

## What do price comparisons leave out?

Income. A city with a low price index is only cheaper to you if your pay does not drop by more than your costs do. Remote workers who keep a higher salary from elsewhere can come out ahead. Workers who must take a local job may find that wages are lower along with prices.

The [MIT Living Wage Calculator](https://livingwage.mit.edu/), from Amy K. Glasmeier and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, helps with this. It estimates the hourly wage a full-time worker needs to cover a family's basic needs in a given county, metro area, or state, for 12 family types that vary by number of adults and children ([MIT Living Wage Calculator](https://livingwage.mit.edu/)). It uses a modest budget with no room for savings or extras, so it sets a floor, not a target ([MIT methodology](https://livingwage.mit.edu/pages/methodology)).

Moving costs, tax differences, and distance from family are not among the factors Visual Capitalist lists for its quality-of-life measure (safety, health care, pollution, traffic, and climate). Each can be worth real money.

## How can a household use these rankings sensibly?

Treat them as a shortlist generator. Pick the two or three places that look interesting, then replace the averages with your own numbers: actual rents on current listings, a realistic salary for your line of work, and the costs that apply to your household, such as child care or a second car.

Rankings of 99 cities cannot know any of that. They can only point at where it might be worth looking.

## One next step

Open the MIT Living Wage Calculator, look up your current county and one you are considering, and compare the required wage for your household size against the pay you could realistically earn in each.
