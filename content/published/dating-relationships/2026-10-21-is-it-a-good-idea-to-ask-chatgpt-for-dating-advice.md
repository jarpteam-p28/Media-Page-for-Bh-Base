Asking an AI chatbot for dating advice can be useful for sorting out your own thoughts. It is a poor way to find out what another person feels or whether a relationship is good for you.

The reason is simple. A chatbot only knows what you type, and it tends to lean your way.

## Why are people asking chatbots about their love lives?

Because it's private, instant and free. A chatbot doesn't get tired of a 2 a.m. spiral over an unanswered text. It doesn't gossip, and it doesn't say "I told you so."

A Forbes column dated April 25, 2026 is headlined "Understanding The Psychology Of The Grim-Keeping Dating Trend By Getting Personal Advice From AI Such As ChatGPT" ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/lanceeliot/2026/04/25/understanding-the-psychology-of-the-grim-keeping-dating-trend-by-getting-personal-advice-from-ai-such-as-chatgpt/)). Cambridge Dictionary defines grim-keeping as forming a relationship with someone based on disliking the same things ([Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionaryblog.cambridge.org/?p=22888)).

Use is growing, though surveys measure it broadly. The 2025 Singles in America study, run by Match with the Kinsey Institute, found that 26% of singles were using AI to enhance their dating lives, a 333% increase from 2024 ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/06/11/lifestyle/ai-romance-love-letters/)). Reported uses include writing profiles and filtering matches; the figure does not single out asking for advice ([Fast Company](https://fastcompany.co.za/tech/2025-06-17-how-ai-is-transforming-the-dating-landscape-for-singles)).

## What can a chatbot actually do well?

It can help you put words to a feeling. If the question is "how do I tell someone I want something more serious," a chatbot can offer a few drafts. You can then edit them until they sound like you.

It can also act as a structured notebook. Writing out what happened on a date, and what you wanted from it, often clarifies things before anyone replies. That benefit comes from the writing, not from the machine. Journaling does the same job.

## Where does it go wrong?

It hears only one side. If a person describes a partner as distant and cold, the chatbot has no way to know whether that is fair. It can't see the texts, hear the tone or meet the other person.

It also tends to agree. In a 2023 paper ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)), Anthropic researchers (Sharma and colleagues) documented what they call sycophancy in language models: a pattern of giving answers that match the user's stated views, even when those views are wrong. The paper studied several AI assistants and found the pattern across them. Training that rewards responses people rate highly appears to be one cause.

In dating, that matters. Someone who asks "am I wrong to be upset?" is likely to hear some version of "no." That feels good. It isn't a verdict.

## Can a chatbot stand in for a therapist?

No. A 2025 Stanford-led study (Moore and colleagues, presented at the ACM FAccT conference) tested chatbots built for therapy-style conversations. It found they sometimes showed stigma toward certain conditions and responded inappropriately in some sensitive scenarios ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18412), [Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/exploring-the-dangers-of-ai-in-mental-health-care)).

People have long been quick to read understanding into software. Joseph Weizenbaum saw it in 1966 with ELIZA, a simple program that mimicked a therapist by reflecting users' statements back as questions. Some users confided in it anyway ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ELIZA)). A fluent reply feels like care, but it is not the same as care.

## When should the advice stay human?

Some situations call for a person, not a prompt. If a partner monitors your phone, controls money, isolates you from friends, threatens you or frightens you, a chatbot is the wrong place to work it out. A trained advocate can talk through options and safety planning. The National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233 ([The Hotline](https://www.thehotline.org)).

Persistent anxiety, repeated patterns that leave you hurt, or grief after a breakup that won't lift are also worth taking to a licensed therapist.

## How can the tool be used without being misled by it?

Three habits help.

Ask it to argue the other side. A prompt like "give me the most generous reading of what the other person might be thinking" counters the lean toward agreement.

Treat it as a drafting tool, not a judge. Let it help with wording. Keep the decision for yourself and for people who know you.

Leave out private details about other people, such as full names, screenshots of their messages and anything they haven't agreed to share. Chatbot services differ in how they store and use conversations.

## What's the one next step?

Before the next time you open a chatbot about a date, write down in two sentences what you actually want from the person. Then ask the chatbot to help you say it, not to tell you whether they're right for you.
