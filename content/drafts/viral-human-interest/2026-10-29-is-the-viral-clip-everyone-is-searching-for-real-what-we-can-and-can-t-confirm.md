## Why do headlines like this keep showing up?

They are built to match what people type into search engines. When a name or phrase starts trending, low-quality sites publish pages stuffed with those exact words, hoping to catch the traffic. The page rarely contains the promised video. Often it contains ads, a redirect, or a prompt to install something.

Security firms have documented this for years. Malwarebytes, for example, has repeatedly written about "viral video" lures on social media and spam sites that lead to scams or malware. [VERIFY: pick one specific Malwarebytes Labs post, with title, date and URL, to cite here.]

## Why does it matter who is named?

Many of these headlines attach a real person's name to a supposed explicit or embarrassing clip. Whether the clip is real, edited, AI-generated or entirely invented is usually impossible to tell from the headline. The harm to the named person is the same either way: their name becomes associated with something sexual or humiliating, searchable by anyone.

Sharing intimate images of someone without consent is illegal in many countries. In Nepal, the Electronic Transactions Act of 2008 and the Privacy Act of 2018 are generally cited as the relevant laws. ## What should a reader do if they see a link like this?

Don't click it, and don't forward it. Forwarding is how these pages spread, and how a name stays attached to a rumor.

If a page has already been opened and asks for a download, a login, or permission to send notifications, close it. Don't approve any of those prompts.

## What if the person in a real clip wants it taken down?

There are tools. StopNCII.org, run by the UK charity SWGfL, lets adults create a digital fingerprint of an intimate image on their own device so participating platforms can block it from being shared. The images are not uploaded. Google also offers a removal request process for non-consensual explicit imagery in search results.
