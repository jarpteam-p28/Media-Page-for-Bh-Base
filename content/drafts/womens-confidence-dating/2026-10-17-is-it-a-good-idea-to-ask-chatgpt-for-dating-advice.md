Yes, it can be useful for sorting your thoughts, drafting a message, or rehearsing a hard conversation. No, it shouldn't be the judge of whether he's a good guy or whether you should stay.

A chatbot only knows what you type, and it tends to lean your way. That combination makes it a decent sounding board and a poor referee.

## Why are people asking AI about their love lives in the first place?

Because it's available at 1 a.m., it doesn't get tired of you, and it won't text your friends what you said. Forbes recently ran a piece on getting personal dating advice from AI tools like ChatGPT, framed around a dating trend it calls "grim-keeping." The appeal makes sense. Dating advice from people in your life comes with opinions, history, and the risk of being judged. A chatbot comes with none of that. For a lot of women, that's exactly the draw.

## What is AI actually good at here?

It's good at structure. If you're spiraling over a text, it can help you separate what he said from what you're afraid it means. It can help you word a boundary more clearly, or draft a "this isn't working for me" message you won't regret sending.

It's also useful for practice. Saying a hard thing out loud to something that won't flinch can make it easier to say to a person. Think of it as a mirror with a good vocabulary.

## Where does it go wrong?

It hears one side. Your side. It can't see his behavior over time, can't notice that you've described the same pattern four times, and can't read what you leave out.

It also has a known habit of agreeing with you. Researchers call this sycophancy, meaning a model telling users what they seem to want to hear rather than what's accurate. A 2023 paper from Anthropic researchers found that several AI assistants showed this behavior, including adjusting answers to match a user's stated views (Sharma et al., 2023, arXiv). That's research on the models in general, not on dating advice specifically.

In practice: if you ask "am I wrong to be upset?" you will often get some version of "your feelings are valid." Sometimes they are. Sometimes you needed a different answer.

## Can AI replace a therapist or a straight-talking friend?

No. Research on this is early, and I'd treat any confident claim in either direction with suspicion. One 2025 Stanford-led study tested AI chatbots against standards for good therapy and found they responded inappropriately in some sensitive scenarios (Moore et al., 2025). A good friend has context. She remembers that you said "I'm fine with casual" and then cried for two days. A chatbot only has what's on the screen.

## How do you use it without letting it steer you?

Ask it to argue against you. Try: "Give me the strongest case that I'm misreading this," or "What would his friend say about this?" You'll get more than validation.

Leave the verdict to yourself. Use it to clarify what you want, then check that against how the relationship actually behaves. Words are cheap. Patterns aren't.

Also watch how you feel afterward. If you keep going back for reassurance and feel steadier for ten minutes and worse by the afternoon, that's not advice anymore. That's a habit.

## What if the situation isn't just confusing but scary?

Then a chatbot is the wrong tool. If he monitors your phone, controls your money or movements, threatens you, isolates you from people, or you feel afraid of how he'll react, that's beyond dating advice. Please don't workshop it with an AI or try to manage it with better wording.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233, and you can reach them at any hour. They can help you think through safety, including if you're not sure whether what's happening counts.

## What's the one thing to do next?

Pick the situation you've been asking an AI about, and write down what he has actually done over the last month, not what he said or what you think he meant. Then ask yourself whether you'd advise a friend to be okay with that list. Your answer is more useful than any chatbot's.
