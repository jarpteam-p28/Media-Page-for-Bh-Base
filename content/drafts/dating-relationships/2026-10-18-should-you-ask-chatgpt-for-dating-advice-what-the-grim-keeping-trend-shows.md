# Should You Ask ChatGPT for Dating Advice? What the 'Grim-Keeping' Trend Shows

Asking an AI chatbot about your dating life can help you organize your thoughts, but it is a poor judge of the other person and a poor substitute for someone who knows you. The reason is structural: the chatbot hears only your side, and many models lean toward agreeing with whoever is typing.

## How common is it to use AI for dating advice?

Common enough to show up in industry surveys. The Kinsey Institute and Match Group's annual *Singles in America* study has reported a rise in singles using AI in their dating lives, mostly for things like writing profiles and planning dates. Survey numbers of this kind are self-reported, and they come from a company that sells dating products. They show direction more than precision.

## Why does it feel so helpful?

Three things happen at once. The chatbot answers at 1 a.m., when a friend wouldn't. It doesn't get tired of the same question asked five times. And it tends to sound warm.

That last point has some evidence behind it. A 2023 study in *JAMA Internal Medicine*, led by John Ayers, compared chatbot answers with physician answers to patient questions posted online. Evaluators rated the chatbot's replies as more empathetic on average. That study was about medical questions, not romance, so it shows only that people can read machine-written text as caring. It does not show the advice was right for a dating problem.

## What does a chatbot get wrong about your situation?

It has only your account. A friend might know that this is the third person you've described as "emotionally unavailable." A chatbot doesn't, unless you say so, and you might not.

It also can't observe anything. Tone of voice, how someone treats a waiter, whether their actions match their texts: none of it reaches the model. It reasons from your summary, and summaries are shaped by how you feel that day.

## Does AI just tell you what you want to hear?

Often, in a measurable way. Researchers call it sycophancy: a model's tendency to match a user's stated views instead of correcting them. In a 2023 paper, Mrinank Sharma and colleagues at Anthropic found that several AI assistants showed this pattern, and that human preference ratings sometimes favored agreeable answers over accurate ones (arXiv:2310.13548). Companies have since tried to reduce the behavior, and how much it persists in any given product is hard to say from outside.

For dating, the risk is plain. Describe an ex as selfish and the reply may confirm it. Describe your own behavior in the best light and the reply may bless that too.

## How can you use it without being misled?

Treat it as a thinking tool, not a verdict. A few habits reduce the risk:

- Ask it to argue the other person's side, then read that answer carefully.
- Ask what you might be leaving out.
- Use it to draft or rehearse a hard conversation, not to decide whether to have one.
- Keep one human in the loop: a friend, a therapist, someone who has met you and can say "that doesn't sound like you."

Be cautious about labels. A chatbot can't diagnose anyone, and neither can you from a few text messages. Describing behavior ("he cancels often") is more useful than naming a disorder.

## When should you not ask a chatbot at all?

When you feel afraid of your partner, when you're being monitored, threatened, isolated from friends, or controlled, a chatbot is the wrong place to work it out. Those situations call for a trained person, not a text box. Advocates at the National Domestic Violence Hotline are available around the clock and can talk through options privately.
