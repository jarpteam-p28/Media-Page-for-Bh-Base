The protests are about the cost of housing and food, low pay, schools and strained public services, and the demands differ from country to country, according to reports by [Euronews](https://euronews.com/2026/10/19/wages-vs-prices-the-economic-pressures-behind-europes-protests) and [the Associated Press](https://www.wcax.com/2026/10/19/france-albania-social-unrest-is-setting-europes-streets-ablaze/) on 19 October 2026. Energy and fuel costs are on that list, but neither report names them as the main cause. For US households any effect is indirect. To show how a European price squeeze can connect to American budgets, this article looks back at the 2022 energy crisis.

## What are people in Europe actually protesting?

The protests do not form a single Europe-wide movement, and their demands differ, [Euronews reported](https://euronews.com/2026/10/19/wages-vs-prices-the-economic-pressures-behind-europes-protests). Rents, food prices, low wages, school conditions and public services all feature. Citing Eurostat data, Euronews reported that EU food prices rose 34% between August 2021 and August 2026.

The sharpest recent price surge came in 2022. Euro area inflation, the yearly rise in consumer prices, hit 10.6% in October 2022, according to [Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/documents/2995521/15265521/2-17112022-AP-EN.pdf/), the European Union's statistics agency. That was the highest monthly reading in [Eurostat's euro area series](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/api/dissemination/statistics/1.0/data/prc_hicp_manr?geo=EA&coicop=CP00&sinceTimePeriod=1997-01), which covers 1997 to 2025. Energy was the largest single driver, adding 4.44 percentage points to the annual rate that month.

## Why did energy get so expensive in Europe?

Europe depended heavily on Russian gas, and Russia cut or reduced supplies to many EU countries after its invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. By late July 2022, 12 EU member states had faced a partial or total cut, according to the European Commission, [Anadolu Agency reported](https://www.aa.com.tr/en/economy/all-eyes-turn-to-russias-share-of-eus-gas-imports/2647905). In 2021, before the war, Russia supplied about 45% of the EU's gas imports and almost 40% of its total gas consumption, according to the [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/a-10-point-plan-to-reduce-the-european-unions-reliance-on-russian-natural-gas).

Gas often sets the price of Europe's electricity. Between 2015 and 2021, gas plants set wholesale power prices about 39% of the time across the EU, Great Britain and Norway, and more than 80% of the time in 2021 in several countries, including Italy and the Netherlands, [according to a 2023 study in Energy Reports](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/the-role-of-natural-gas-in-setting-electricity-prices-in-europe/). In European wholesale markets, the highest cleared bid, usually from the most expensive power plant needed to meet demand, sets the price for everyone, as the European Commission's [Joint Research Centre explains](https://ses.jrc.ec.europa.eu/index.php/publications-list/redesigning-european-electricity-market). So when gas prices spike, wholesale electricity prices follow, and bills can rise even in homes that never use gas, depending on how closely a household's tariff tracks wholesale prices.

## Is the US in the same position?

Not quite. The US produces more natural gas than it uses: in 2022, US dry natural gas production was about 13% higher than total US consumption, according to the [US Energy Information Administration](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/natural-gas/where-our-natural-gas-comes-from.php) (EIA). The EIA says US gas prices are affected by [market supply and demand](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/natural-gas/factors-affecting-natural-gas-prices.php), and lists import and export volumes among the supply factors. So the US did not depend on imported gas the way Europe did.

They still felt inflation. US consumer prices rose 9.1% in the 12 months to June 2022, the largest increase since 1981, according to the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_07132022.htm). Energy prices rose 41.6% and food prices 10.4% over that year, shelter rose 5.6%, new vehicles 11.4% and used cars and trucks 7.1%, the same release shows.

## How does Europe's squeeze reach American households?

Through a few channels, none of them dramatic on their own. Europe's demand for liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled into liquid so it can be shipped) pulled US exports upward. The EU and UK took 64% of US LNG exports in the first five months of 2022, and the US became the world's largest LNG exporter in the first half of that year, [according to the EIA](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=53159). The EIA [lists increased exports, especially LNG](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/natural-gas/factors-affecting-natural-gas-prices.php), as one reason US natural gas prices rose in 2019 and each year through 2022.

Weaker European spending can also hurt US exporters and companies with European sales. And a tense energy market can raise shipping and manufacturing costs that eventually show up on shelves. This article does not try to measure how large these effects are.

## What does this mean for a household budget?

The lesson from Europe is about exposure. A household is more exposed to an energy price shock when energy is a large share of its spending and when its bills track wholesale prices closely.

Most US households can check this in a few minutes. Look at whether your electricity or gas rate is fixed or variable, and how much your bill swings between winter and summer. A household with a variable rate and a large seasonal swing is more exposed to a price shock than one on a fixed plan.

## What is one thing to do this week?

Pull your last 12 months of energy bills and note the highest and lowest months. The gap between them shows how much room your budget needs for a bad stretch, and it gives you the numbers to ask your utility about a fixed rate or budget billing, if either is offered where you live.
