## What are people in Europe actually protesting?

The common thread is the gap between prices and paychecks. When the cost of heating, electricity, groceries and rent climbs faster than wages, households cut spending first and organize second.

Euro area inflation, the yearly rise in consumer prices, hit 10.6% in October 2022, according to Eurostat, the European Union's statistics agency. [VERIFY: confirm the 10.6% figure against the specific Eurostat October 2022 release.] [VERIFY: confirm against Eurostat's series that this was a record high for the euro area.] That was the highest reading since the euro area began keeping the measure. Energy was the largest single driver. [VERIFY: confirm with Eurostat's breakdown by component.]

## Why did energy get so expensive in Europe?

Europe depended heavily on Russian pipeline gas, and that supply was disrupted after Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. [VERIFY: source the dependence and supply disruption claims.] Before the war, Russia supplied a large share of the EU's natural gas, according to the European Commission. [VERIFY: the roughly 40% figure may describe consumption rather than imports (imports may be closer to 45%). Check the Commission's REPowerEU material or IEA for the exact share, year, and wording.]

Gas sets the price of much of Europe's electricity. In many European markets, the most expensive power plant needed to meet demand sets the price for everyone. So when gas spikes, electricity bills spike with it, even for homes that never use gas. [VERIFY: source marginal pricing (e.g. European Commission or ACER); the effect on bills depends on each household's tariff.]

## Is the US in the same position?

Not quite. The US produces most of the natural gas it uses, and its gas prices are set largely by domestic supply and demand. [VERIFY: confirm with US Energy Information Administration data.] American households did not face the same cutoff risk.

They still felt inflation. US consumer prices peaked at 9.1% year over year in June 2022, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. [VERIFY: confirm against the BLS June 2022 CPI release.] Food, rent, and vehicles drove much of that, alongside energy. [VERIFY: confirm with the BLS component breakdown.]

## How does Europe's squeeze reach American households?

Through a few channels, none of them dramatic on their own. Europe's demand for liquefied natural gas (LNG, gas cooled into liquid so it can be shipped) pulled US exports upward, which can tie domestic gas prices more closely to world markets. [VERIFY: no data found; check US Energy Information Administration data on US LNG export growth and any measured effect on domestic prices, or cut this claim.]

Weaker European spending can also hurt US exporters and companies with European sales. And a tense energy market can raise shipping and manufacturing costs that eventually show up on shelves. The size of these effects is hard to isolate, and economists disagree on how much each one matters.

## What does this mean for a household budget?

The lesson from Europe is about exposure. Households hit hardest were those where energy was a large share of spending and where bills tracked wholesale prices closely.

Most US households can check this in a few minutes. Look at whether your electricity or gas rate is fixed or variable, and how much your bill swings between winter and summer. A household with a variable rate and a large seasonal swing is more exposed to a price shock than one on a fixed plan.

## What is one thing to do this week?

Pull your last 12 months of energy bills and note the highest and lowest months. The gap between them shows how much room your budget needs for a bad stretch, and it gives you the numbers to ask your utility about a fixed rate or budget billing, if either is offered where you live.
