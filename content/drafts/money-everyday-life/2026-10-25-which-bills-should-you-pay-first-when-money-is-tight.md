When money is tight, pay for the things that keep you housed, fed, warm, and able to get to work first. Debts that can wait longer, such as credit cards and medical bills, come after that.

The order isn't about which bill is biggest or which lender is loudest. It is about which missed payment does the most damage, the fastest, to your daily life. That is the question worth asking of every bill on the table.

## What counts as essential?

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), the federal agency that oversees lenders, publishes a [prioritizing bills worksheet](https://www.kansaslegalservices.org/sites/kansaslegalservices.org/files/cfpb_your-money-your-goals_prioritizing_bills_tool_2018-11_ADA_0.pdf) built on this idea. It tells readers to identify what they need to pay to protect their housing and income, keep their insurance, and meet any court-ordered obligations. Its list includes rent or mortgage, gas, electric and water bills, transportation to get to work, child care, and car, health, and renters or home insurance.

The test is simple. If losing it would put you out of your home, cut off your heat, or stop you reaching your job, it goes first.

## Why does the order matter?

Different debts have different consequences, and the consequences arrive on different timelines.

Missing rent can bring a late fee and the risk of eviction, the CFPB worksheet notes, and how fast an eviction can move depends on state law and the lease. A car loan is secured, meaning the lender can take the car if you stop paying. In many states, once you are in default, and a missed payment is a typical trigger, the lender may be able to repossess the car at any time without notice, according to the [Federal Trade Commission](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/vehicle-repossession). The contract and state law set the details. Losing the car can mean losing the job that pays everything else.

A credit card is different. It is unsecured, meaning there is no house or car behind it. A late payment can bring a late fee, a negative entry on your credit record, lower credit scores, and higher interest rates, according to the CFPB worksheet. Those costs are real. But the lender can't simply take your property. Most creditors can garnish wages only after a court issues a judgment, according to the [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-take-or-garnish-my-wages-or-benefits-en-1439/). There are exceptions: federal and state agencies, such as the IRS, can sometimes garnish pay without a court order, and states can generally garnish wages for child support.

## Is a mortgage payment more urgent than a credit card?

Yes, though a mortgage gives more breathing room than many people assume. Under federal mortgage servicing rules, a servicer generally cannot start foreclosure until a loan is more than 120 days delinquent ([CFPB, Regulation X, 12 CFR 1024.41](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1024/41/)). That window exists so borrowers can ask for help, such as a loan modification or a repayment plan.

The 120 days is not a free pass. Interest, late fees, and the missed payments keep stacking up. It is time to use, not time to wait out.

## What can wait, and what does waiting cost?

Credit card minimums, personal loans, store cards, and medical bills usually sit lower on the list. Medical bills often have room to negotiate. Federal tax rules require nonprofit hospitals to have a written [financial assistance policy](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/financial-assistance-policy-and-emergency-medical-care-policy-section-501r4) covering emergency and other medically necessary care, so it is worth asking about it, and about a payment plan.

Lower priority does not mean ignorable. Interest keeps accruing, and accounts that go far enough past due can be sent to collections. The practical goal is to pay the minimum on these if you can, and to call the lender before you miss a payment instead of after.

## What should you do before you fall behind?

Call early. Lenders, landlords, and utility companies generally have more options for someone who contacts them before a due date than for someone who goes silent. Those options include payment plans, hardship programs, and short deferrals.

Outside help exists too. The federal Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) helps eligible households with heating and cooling bills. Dialing 211, or visiting 211.org, connects callers to local help with rent, food, and utilities. Nonprofit credit counseling agencies can also help build a plan. Fees and quality vary, so check an agency's credentials first.

Many households have little cushion for any of this. In the Federal Reserve's latest [Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20260513a.htm), released in May 2026 and covering 2025, 63 percent of adults said they would cover a $400 emergency expense using cash or its equivalent, which leaves more than a third who would not. Being short in a given month is common, not a personal failing.

## What is the one next step?

Write down every bill you owe on a single page. Next to each, note the due date and what happens if it is late: eviction, shutoff, repossession, a fee, or a credit mark. Then pay from the top of the consequence list down, and call the creditor on anything you can't cover.
