When money is tight, pay for the things that keep you housed, fed, warm, and able to get to work first. Debts that can wait longer, such as credit cards and medical bills, come after that.

The order isn't about which bill is biggest or which lender is loudest. It is about which missed payment does the most damage, the fastest, to your daily life. That is the question behind a recent Washington Post piece headlined "This is what you should pay for first when money is tight," and it is the one worth asking of every bill on the table.

## What counts as essential?

The consumer-finance guidance that agencies and nonprofit credit counselors tend to give is consistent. Essentials are housing (rent or mortgage), utilities, food, basic transportation, health insurance or necessary medications, and child care if you need it to work. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), the federal agency that oversees lenders, groups its advice on prioritizing bills around the same idea. The test is simple. If losing it would put you out of your home, cut off your heat, or stop you reaching your job, it goes first.

## Why does the order matter?

Different debts have different consequences, and the consequences arrive on different timelines.

Missing rent can lead to an eviction filing within weeks in many places, though the timeline depends on the state and the lease. A car loan is secured, meaning the lender can take the car if you stop paying. In many states, repossession can follow soon after a missed payment, depending on the contract and state law. [VERIFY: confirm general statement about repossession timing with a CFPB or FTC source.] Losing the car can mean losing the job that pays everything else.

A credit card is different. It is unsecured, meaning there is no house or car behind it. A missed payment brings late fees, a higher interest rate, and eventually credit-score damage and collection calls. Those costs are real. But the lender can't simply take your property. To garnish wages over a private debt, a creditor generally has to sue and win a court judgment first. Exceptions include certain federal debts, taxes, and child support. [VERIFY: confirm with FTC or CFPB guidance on wage garnishment.]

## Is a mortgage payment more urgent than a credit card?

Yes, though a mortgage gives more breathing room than many people assume. Under federal mortgage servicing rules, a servicer generally cannot start foreclosure until a loan is more than 120 days delinquent (CFPB, Regulation X, 12 CFR 1024.41). That window exists so borrowers can ask for help, such as a loan modification or a repayment plan.

The 120 days is not a free pass. Interest, late fees, and the missed payments keep stacking up. It is time to use, not time to wait out.

## What can wait, and what does waiting cost?

Credit card minimums, personal loans, store cards, and medical bills usually sit lower on the list. Medical bills often have room to negotiate. Many hospitals have financial assistance programs, and a bill can often be put on a payment plan. [VERIFY: cite a source on nonprofit hospital charity-care requirements or CFPB guidance on medical billing.]

Lower priority does not mean ignorable. Interest keeps accruing, and accounts that go far enough past due can be sent to collections. The practical goal is to pay the minimum on these if you can, and to call the lender before you miss a payment instead of after.

## What should you do before you fall behind?

Call early. Lenders, landlords, and utility companies generally have more options for someone who contacts them before a due date than for someone who goes silent. Those options include payment plans, hardship programs, and short deferrals.

Outside help exists too. The federal Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP) helps eligible households with heating and cooling bills. Dialing 211, or visiting 211.org, connects callers to local help with rent, food, and utilities. Nonprofit credit counseling agencies can also help build a plan. Fees and quality vary, so check an agency's credentials first.

Many households have little cushion for any of this. The Federal Reserve's annual survey of household well-being has repeatedly found that a sizable minority of American adults could not cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. [VERIFY: pull the exact percentage and year from the latest Fed "Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households" report.] Being short in a given month is common, not a personal failing.

## What is the one next step?

Write down every bill you owe on a single page. Next to each, note the due date and what happens if it is late: eviction, shutoff, repossession, a fee, or a credit mark. Then pay from the top of the consequence list down, and call the creditor on anything you can't cover.
