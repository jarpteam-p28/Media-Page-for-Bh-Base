Prices feel high because most of them still are. In September 2026, overall prices were 3.7% higher than a year earlier, according to the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.nr0.htm), while hourly pay, after inflation, fell. Gas has started to ease in October, which lifted the mood a little, but a small drop from a high level still leaves the bill high.

## What did the September inflation report actually say?

The Consumer Price Index, released by the BLS on October 14, 2026, showed prices up 0.6% from August and 3.7% from September 2025. [CNN reported](https://www.cnn.com/2026/10/14/economy/us-cpi-inflation-september) that the annual rate was up from 3.4% in August and above what economists expected.

Gas did much of the damage. The BLS gasoline index rose 9.1% in September alone and 34.2% over the year. Energy overall was up 20.4% from a year earlier.

But it was not only gas. "Core" prices, which leave out food and energy, rose 2.6% over the year. Other September figures from the same BLS release:

- **Shelter (rent and housing costs):** up 3.2% over the year, and 0.4% in September alone
- **Electricity:** up 4.4% over the year
- **Restaurant meals:** up 3.5% over the year
- **Groceries:** up 1.8% over the year, and down 0.1% from August

CNN noted price increases in car repairs, airfare, doctor visits, clothing and computers. Gregory Daco, chief economist at EY-Parthenon, told CNN that core goods prices rose at the fastest rate since January 2025.

## Why doesn't my paycheck feel like it's keeping up?

Because, on average, it isn't. The BLS [Real Earnings report](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/realer.nr0.htm) for September, released October 14, 2026, found that average hourly earnings adjusted for inflation fell 0.5% from August and 0.7% from September 2025. Pay went up a little in dollars, but prices went up more.

Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, put it to CNN this way: "We're in a world now of inflation at 3.7% and wage gains for the typical American at 3%; that's a big gap."

A household can see gas tick down and still be further behind than a year ago.

## Is gas really getting cheaper?

A little. The U.S. Energy Information Administration's [weekly survey](https://www.eia.gov/petroleum/gasdiesel/) put the average price of regular gasoline at $4.331 a gallon in the week of October 12, 2026, and $4.269 in the week of October 26. That is a drop of about six cents. The same survey shows the October 26 price was still $1.234 a gallon higher than a year earlier.

Diesel is moving the other way. The EIA's average on-highway diesel price rose from $6.179 a gallon on October 12 to $6.352 on October 26. That matters even for people who never buy diesel. CNN reported that economists expect sharply higher diesel prices could push up food costs.

## Why do surveys say people feel both better and worse?

The two big consumer surveys measure slightly different things, and both point to prices.

The [Conference Board](https://www.conference-board.org/topics/consumer-confidence) said on October 27, 2026, that its Consumer Confidence Index rose to 91.7 in October, from a revised 84.2 in September. It said the recovery was in line with gas prices easing from September to October. But the same release said consumers "continued to express frustration with rising costs," with more write-in mentions of grocery, food and diesel prices. People also expected inflation of 5.8% over the next 12 months, and planned to spend less on services and big-ticket items over the next six months.

The University of Michigan's [Surveys of Consumers](https://www.sca.isr.umich.edu/) tells a gloomier story. Its final October 2026 sentiment index was 48.0, almost unchanged from 48.1 in September and down from 53.6 in October 2025. Survey director Joanne Hsu wrote that "purchasing power continues to dominate consumer views of the economy." Michigan's year-ahead inflation expectation rose to 4.7%, from 4.6% in September.

Put simply, people feel a bit better that gas has stopped climbing. They still feel squeezed, because many other prices have not come back down.

## What can a household do right now?

None of these steps fix inflation, but each one gives a budget more room.

- **Find the three biggest line items.** For most households that means rent or mortgage, food and transportation. Look at the last two months of bank or card statements and total each one.
- **Shift food spending toward the grocery store.** In September, restaurant prices rose 3.5% over the year and grocery prices 1.8%, per the BLS. Long told CNN many families are trading down to store brands and eating a little less meat to cover their bills.
- **Price-check gas before filling up.** With prices drifting down slowly, stations near each other can differ.
- **Stock up on shelf-stable staples while groceries are flat.** If diesel costs do reach food prices, as economists told CNN they might, buying a few weeks of rice, beans, canned goods or frozen vegetables now can soften the next increase. Buy only what will actually get used.
- **Look at any debt with a rate that can change.** CNN reported that the September data could push the Federal Reserve toward raising interest rates. Credit cards and other variable-rate debt usually follow. Paying down the highest-rate balance first, or calling the card company to ask about a lower rate, can save money either way.

## What should a reader watch next?

The next monthly inflation report from the BLS will cover October. Whether gas keeps easing, and whether diesel shows up in grocery prices, will decide whether that report feels like relief or more of the same.
