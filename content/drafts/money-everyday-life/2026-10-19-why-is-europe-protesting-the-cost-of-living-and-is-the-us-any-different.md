People in several European countries took to the streets over the cost of living because prices rose faster than paychecks, and energy and food bills did not fall back when inflation cooled. US households are under the same kind of squeeze, though the details differ.

Al Jazeera reported protests in Belgium, Italy, Portugal and France, and Euronews described the economic pressures behind them. [VERIFY: publication dates of the Al Jazeera, CNBC and Euronews pieces. The French unrest CNBC describes may be a separate episode from the cost-of-living marches, and the two should not be merged into one story.]

## What are the protesters actually angry about?

The common thread is the gap between what things cost and what people earn. Unions and workers in these protests have generally pressed for wages that keep up with prices, and for relief on energy and food costs. [VERIFY: specific demands and turnout figures from the Al Jazeera report.]

Energy is a big part of the European story. After Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, natural gas prices in Europe surged, and household heating and electricity bills followed. [VERIFY: peak wholesale gas price and date, e.g. Dutch TTF benchmark, August 2022.]

## Is this just inflation?

Mostly, but the word hides two different things. Inflation is the rate at which prices rise. The price level is where prices sit after those rises.

Eurozone inflation peaked at roughly 10 percent in autumn 2022, according to Eurostat. [VERIFY: exact October 2022 figure, reported at 10.6% in Eurostat's release.] US consumer price inflation peaked at 9.1 percent for the 12 months ending June 2022, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. [VERIFY: confirm the 9.1% figure on bls.gov.]

When the rate falls, prices stop climbing so fast. They do not fall back. A household that pays a lot more for groceries and power than it did a few years ago keeps paying it. That is why anger can persist even after the headline number improves.

## Why do wages lag?

Wages tend to be reset slowly, often once a year or under multi-year contracts, while prices can change in weeks. In a sudden price jump, real wages, meaning pay after adjusting for inflation, can fall for a while even if paychecks are growing.

The OECD, the club of mostly wealthy economies, reported in 2023 that real wages were falling in most of its member countries. [VERIFY: OECD Employment Outlook 2023, exact figure and period.] Whether wages have since caught up varies by country and by job.

## Does this apply to US households?

In shape, yes. In degree, it varies. US wage growth did outpace inflation for some workers in 2023 and later, but the evidence is mixed across income groups. [VERIFY: BLS real average hourly earnings data, with date.]

US households also feel a different set of pressures. Housing, health insurance, childcare and car costs tend to weigh more here, while Europe's protests have centered more on energy and food. [VERIFY: this comparison against BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey and Eurostat household spending data.]

## What can a household do with this?

Compare, don't average. National inflation figures describe a typical basket of goods, and no household is typical. A renter with a long commute and a homeowner with a fixed-rate mortgage are living through different inflation.

The most useful move is to look at your own numbers, starting with the categories that rose the most.

**Next step:** Pull your last 12 months of bank and card statements, list your five biggest recurring costs, and note which ones rose the most compared with two years ago. Start with that one.
