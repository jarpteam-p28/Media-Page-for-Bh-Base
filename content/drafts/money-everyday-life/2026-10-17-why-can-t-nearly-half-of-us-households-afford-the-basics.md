Roughly four in ten US households earn too little to cover a bare-bones budget where they live, even though most of them have a job. [VERIFY: the 'four in ten' figure matches ALICE's 42%, but no source was found for 'most of them have a job'.] The gap comes mostly from the price of a few big essentials rising faster than the paychecks meant to cover them.

A recent New York Times story, "Rising Costs Push Necessities Out of Reach for Nearly Half of Americans," brought the issue back into the news. It reports on a Pew Research Center survey, not ALICE data: Pew found that 44% of adults had trouble paying for a basic necessity in the past year ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/10/14/many-americans-are-struggling-to-afford-basic-necessities/)). That is a self-reported measure, different from ALICE's count below. A widely cited count of this problem comes from United Way's ALICE project.

## What does "can't afford the basics" actually measure?

ALICE stands for Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed. It describes households that earn more than the federal poverty line but less than the local cost of basic living. [VERIFY: confirm the ALICE definition on unitedforalice.org; the checker did not open a page confirming it.]

United Way's researchers build what they call a household survival budget for each county. It covers housing, child care, food, transportation, health care, a basic phone and internet plan, and taxes. It leaves out savings, restaurants, and vacations. [VERIFY: confirm the Household Survival Budget components against the ALICE methodology; this also affects 'seven categories' in the closing advice.]

In United Way's ALICE reports, about 42% of US households fell below that line in recent data years. [VERIFY: second check disagreed: The ALICE PDF could not be opened, so the 42% figure and the 2023 data year are unverified. A human should check the report.] That group includes households officially in poverty and a larger group who sit just above it.

The official poverty line is the reason the number looks so high. It was designed in the 1960s around the cost of food ([Social Security Bulletin](https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v68n3/v68n3p79.html)).

## Why do costs outrun paychecks?

Two different things get blurred together: the inflation rate and the price level. The inflation rate is how fast prices are rising. The price level is where prices stand after years of increases.

When inflation cools, the rate falls but prices don't. Grocery prices rose 1.8% over the 12 months to September 2026 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm)), but that is growth on top of earlier increases. The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks this through the [Consumer Price Index](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/). [VERIFY: cumulative CPI change from January 2020 to the most recent month (Sept 2026 CPI-U was 336.940, released 14 Oct 2026); the January 2020 index level was not checked.]

Wages have recovered some of the ground. In some recent periods they rose faster than prices. [VERIFY: compare current BLS average hourly earnings growth with CPI growth (12-month CPI was 3.7% in Sept 2026, up from 3.4% in Aug); this line may be out of date.] But catching up with last year's prices does not undo the jump from three years ago, and households with little cushion felt that jump most.

## Which costs do the most damage?

Housing is the largest line in nearly every household budget. Rent and home prices have risen in most parts of the country, and a family moving to a new place usually pays more than the family that left.

Child care is the second shock for households with young children. In most states, a year of care for one infant costs more than a year of in-state public college tuition, according to Child Care Aware of America's [2025 Price & Supply report](https://info.childcareaware.org/media/child-care-prices-rival-major-household-expenses), which puts the national average annual price of child care at $13,184.

Food, car costs, and health care add to the pile. Car repair costs have climbed noticeably: the CPI for motor vehicle maintenance and repair rose 5.8% over the 12 months to September 2026 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cpi.htm)).

None of these can be skipped. That is why a raise of a few percent can still leave a household behind: the bills that grew fastest are the ones with no off switch.

## Why does a thin cushion make it worse?

A household that is just covering the basics has no room for a surprise. The Federal Reserve's annual Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking …has found that 63% of adults could cover a $400 emergency expense using cash or its equivalent in the 2025 survey, unchanged from 2024 ([Federal Reserve Board](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20260513a.htm)); the remaining roughly 37% could not or would have to borrow or sell something.

When a car breaks down or a medical bill arrives, that gap goes onto a credit card or a missed payment. Interest and late fees then make the next month harder. The pattern is a mechanism, not a character flaw: a small shock meets a small buffer.

## What can a household do with this information?

The data describes a structural squeeze, so no budgeting trick fully closes it. But knowing where a household sits relative to its local costs changes what to tackle first. Housing and child care usually matter more than daily spending habits, and they're also the hardest to move.

The one step worth taking: look up your county's household survival budget at unitedforalice.org, then compare it line by line to what you actually spend on those seven categories. The biggest gap shows where to focus first, whether that's a rent conversation, a child care subsidy, or a benefits check.
