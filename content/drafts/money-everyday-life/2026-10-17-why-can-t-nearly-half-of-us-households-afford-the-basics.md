Roughly four in ten US households earn too little to cover a bare-bones budget where they live, even though most of them have a job. The gap comes mostly from the price of a few big essentials rising faster than the paychecks meant to cover them.

A recent New York Times story, "Rising Costs Push Necessities Out of Reach for Nearly Half of Americans," brought the issue back into the news. ## What does "can't afford the basics" actually measure?

ALICE stands for Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed. It describes households that earn more than the federal poverty line but less than the local cost of basic living.

United Way's researchers build what they call a household survival budget for each county. It covers housing, child care, food, transportation, health care, a basic phone and internet plan, and taxes. It leaves out savings, restaurants, and vacations.

In United Way's ALICE reports, about 42% of US households fell below that line in recent data years. [VERIFY: exact percentage and data year in the latest ALICE national report, United Way, unitedforalice.org.] That group includes households officially in poverty and a larger group who sit just above it.

The official poverty line is the reason the number looks so high. It was designed in the 1960s around food costs and doesn't reflect what housing or child care cost today. [VERIFY: Census Bureau/Orshansky history of the poverty threshold.]

## Why do costs outrun paychecks?

Two different things get blurred together: the inflation rate and the price level. The inflation rate is how fast prices are rising. The price level is where prices stand after years of increases.

When inflation cools, the rate falls but prices don't. A grocery bill that jumped in 2021 and 2022 stays high in 2025 even if it's now growing slowly. The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks this through the Consumer Price Index (bls.gov/cpi). [VERIFY: cumulative CPI change from January 2020 to the most recent month, with the BLS release date.]

Wages have recovered some of the ground. In some recent periods they rose faster than prices. [VERIFY: current BLS average hourly earnings growth against CPI growth, with dates.] But catching up with last year's prices does not undo the jump from three years ago, and households with little cushion felt that jump most.

## Which costs do the most damage?

Housing is the largest line in nearly every household budget. Rent and home prices have risen in most parts of the country, and a family moving to a new place usually pays more than the family that left.

Child care is the second shock for households with young children. In many states, a year of care for one infant costs more than a year of in-state public college tuition, according to Child Care Aware of America's annual reports. [VERIFY: current report year and the specific states or national average cited.]

Food, car costs, and health care add to the pile. Auto insurance and car repair have both climbed noticeably in recent years. [VERIFY: BLS CPI motor vehicle insurance index change, with dates.]

None of these can be skipped. That is why a raise of a few percent can still leave a household behind: the bills that grew fastest are the ones with no off switch.

## Why does a thin cushion make it worse?

A household that is just covering the basics has no room for a surprise. The Federal Reserve's annual Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking has found that roughly one in three adults could not cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. [VERIFY: the share from the most recent SHED report, Federal Reserve Board, published each May; in the 2023 survey about 63% said they could.]

When a car breaks down or a medical bill arrives, that gap goes onto a credit card or a missed payment. Interest and late fees then make the next month harder. The pattern is a mechanism, not a character flaw: a small shock meets a small buffer.

## What can a household do with this information?

The data describes a structural squeeze, so no budgeting trick fully closes it. But knowing where a household sits relative to its local costs changes what to tackle first. Housing and child care usually matter more than daily spending habits, and they're also the hardest to move.

The one step worth taking: look up your county's household survival budget at unitedforalice.org, then compare it line by line to what you actually spend on those seven categories. The biggest gap shows where to focus first, whether that's a rent conversation, a child care subsidy, or a benefits check.
