Prices still feel high because a falling inflation rate does not bring prices back down. It only means they are rising more slowly than before. The same thing is playing out on both sides of the Atlantic, and Europe's recent affordability protests are a visible example.

## What is happening in France, Italy, Spain and Portugal?

Cost of living has become a political problem across southern Europe. Al Jazeera recently ran an explainer asking how it has changed in France, Italy, Spain and Portugal. Business Insider and DW have covered protests in France framed around affordability.

The details differ by country. The pattern is familiar to Americans: a sharp price jump, then a slowdown that brings little relief.

## Why does cooling inflation not feel like relief?

Inflation is a rate of change. If a basket of groceries costs $100 and inflation is 5% for a year, it costs $105. If inflation then drops to 2%, it costs $107.10. The price keeps rising, just more gently.

Prices only fall when there is deflation, which is rare and usually comes with economic trouble. So households carry the earlier increases permanently, unless wages catch up.

Individual countries ran differently. Italy and Spain each saw double-digit annual rates at some point in 2022 by Eurostat's harmonized measure. [VERIFY: peak rates and months for Italy, Spain, France and Portugal before stating any country figure.]

Europe was hit especially hard by energy. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine disrupted natural gas supplies, and many European households faced much larger heating and electricity bills.

## Why does it matter whether wages keep up?

What decides how a household feels is the gap between price growth and pay growth. If prices rise 15% over a few years and pay rises 10%, the household is worse off even though both numbers went up.

This is why affordability anger can persist after headline inflation fades. Rent, food and utilities take a large share of a budget, so increases there are noticed first and forgiven last. Housing is a major piece. Economists generally find that lower-income households spend a bigger share of income on necessities, so they feel the same price increase more heavily. ## Is the US situation the same?

In outline, yes. US prices are well above their early-2020 level, and the BLS publishes the exact figures. [VERIFY: current cumulative CPI change since January 2020 from the BLS calculator, with date.] Whether your own budget is better or worse depends on your pay, your rent or mortgage, and what you buy most.

The national average is a poor guide to your household. Someone who rents in a fast-rising market and drives a lot will have a different experience than a homeowner with a fixed-rate mortgage.

## What can a household actually do with this?

Measure your own inflation rather than relying on the national number. The headline rate averages everyone's spending, and yours is not average.

One concrete step: pull your bills for your three biggest spending categories (usually housing, food and transportation) from three years ago and today. Then run the old amounts through the BLS CPI Inflation Calculator at bls.gov/data/inflation_calculator.htm. If a category rose far faster than the calculator suggests, that is where to look for savings, a renegotiation or a switch.
