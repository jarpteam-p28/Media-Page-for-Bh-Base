An enzyme bath is a warm soak in fermenting plant material, and the part that most likely does something is the heat. The claims built around the "enzymes" are much less supported, and no good trial of the baths themselves appears to exist.

The practice is being covered as Korea's latest wellness trend. The Korea Herald reported on it under the headline "Bury yourself in enzyme baths." Versions of it have been offered for decades in Japan, where it is often called *kōso-yoku*. ## What is an enzyme bath?

Guests lie in a tub or pit filled with a warm mix, typically cedar sawdust or wood chips, often with rice bran, then get covered with more of it. Microbes break down the material, and that fermentation gives off heat. [VERIFY: typical composition and temperature range, often reported as roughly 50 to 60°C in Japanese venues; confirm with a primary source or the Korea Herald article.]

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction. Fermentation depends on enzymes, which is where the name comes from. It does not mean the bath delivers enzymes into your body.

## What do enzyme baths claim to do?

Operators and promotional coverage tend to describe the baths as detoxifying, circulation-boosting, skin-improving, and good for stress. These are common claims across spa traditions. They are usually stated broadly, without a defined outcome that could be tested.

## What does the evidence actually show?

For the enzyme baths specifically, very little. A search for controlled human trials turns up few or none in the English-language literature. [VERIFY: run a PubMed search for "enzyme bath" and "kōso-yoku" to confirm that no controlled trials exist.]

That leaves the evidence on heat, which is a different question. The best-known work is on saunas. A 2015 study in *JAMA Internal Medicine* followed Finnish men and found that more frequent sauna use was associated with lower rates of fatal cardiovascular events and death from all causes. The study was observational. It cannot show that the sauna caused the benefit, since people who use saunas often may differ in other ways. A fermenting bath is also not a sauna: the heat is delivered differently, through contact with the body rather than hot air.

What can reasonably be said is that warm-water and heat exposure can make people feel relaxed and can temporarily relieve muscle tension. Whether an enzyme bath adds anything beyond a hot bath or sauna is untested.

## Do the enzymes get into your body?

There is no good evidence that they do. Enzymes are large molecules, and intact skin is a barrier built to keep such molecules out. Some skincare products use enzymes such as papain, from papaya, to loosen dead surface skin cells. That effect happens on the surface and depends on the formulation. It does not show that a bath of fermenting sawdust affects anything deeper. [VERIFY: whether any published study measures absorption or skin effects from fermented-bran or cedar baths.]

"Detox" claims are the weakest of all. The liver and kidneys handle waste removal, and no study reviewed for this draft shows that sweating in a bath removes meaningful amounts of any harmful substance. [VERIFY: cite a review of sweat and toxin excretion if this point is kept.]

## Who should be careful?

Any hot immersion carries risks, and burying the body in insulating material may raise them, since it can be harder to cool off or to leave quickly. Heat can cause dizziness, fainting, and dehydration. People with heart conditions, low blood pressure, or who are pregnant are commonly advised to check with a clinician before using saunas or hot baths. Alcohol makes heat tolerance worse. Some medications, including certain diuretics and blood pressure drugs, can change how the body handles heat. There is also a hygiene question. The bath material is a living, fermenting mix. People with open wounds, skin infections, or weakened immune systems have reason to ask how the material is handled and replaced. Dust from wood or bran may bother people with asthma or allergies. No safety data specific to enzyme baths were found for this draft. [VERIFY: any published safety reports or regulatory guidance in Korea or Japan.]

## So is it worth trying?

As a warm, quiet hour, probably yes for healthy adults, with the same caution that applies to any hot bath. As a treatment for anything, there is no evidence to support it. It should not replace or delay medical care, and it is no reason to change prescribed medication.

A practical next step: before booking a session, ask the venue what the bath temperature is, how often the material is replaced, and how long a session lasts. If you have a heart condition, are pregnant, or take regular medication, ask your doctor first.
