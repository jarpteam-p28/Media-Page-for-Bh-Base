Oil pulling, swishing a spoonful of oil around the mouth for several minutes and then spitting it out, has some research behind it, but not much, and most of that research is weak. Short, small trials suggest it may lower the number of bacteria in saliva. There is no good evidence that it beats a normal routine of brushing with fluoride toothpaste and flossing, and the American Dental Association does not recommend it.

## Why is oil pulling in the news again?

On October 28, 2026, CNN examined the trend under the headline [“‘Oil pulling’: Natural, stronger mouthwash or waste of time?”](https://www.cnn.com/2026/10/28/health/oil-pulling-wellness) [VERIFY: the CNN article's full text could not be loaded for this draft, so no claims or quotes from it are used here; a human should read it and confirm its conclusion about toothpaste.]

The practice is not new, and it keeps returning through short videos and wellness posts.

## What does oil pulling claim to do?

The method is simple: a spoonful of coconut, sesame or another edible oil, swished around the mouth and then spat out. Online claims go well beyond fresh breath. In 2023, [PolitiFact checked a Facebook video](https://www.politifact.com/factchecks/2023/apr/28/facebook-posts/no-hard-evidence-oil-pulling-improves-oral-health/) saying a daily 20-minute rinse with virgin coconut oil would strengthen gums, stop cavities, whiten teeth and cut plaque. PolitiFact rated the claim Mostly False.

## What does the research actually show?

A large review by Jong, Ooi and Teoh, was [published in the *International Journal of Dental Hygiene*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37635453/) (online in 2023, in print in 2024). It gathered 25 randomized trials with 1,184 participants and pooled 21 of them.

The findings were mixed:

- Compared with ordinary, non-chlorhexidine mouthwashes, oil pulling improved one measure of gum inflammation, the modified gingival index.
- Chlorhexidine, a prescription-strength antiseptic rinse, reduced plaque more than oil pulling did.
- The authors rated the overall quality of the evidence as "very low."

A [summary by the Dental Elf](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/dentistry/oral-health-other-health-conditions/oil-pulling-oral-health/), an evidence-based dentistry blog, adds context. Of the 25 trials, 24 were at high risk of bias in at least one area. They were small, with 20 to 90 people each, and lasted no more than 30 days. The gum benefit over regular mouthwash rested on just two studies. Sesame and coconut oil were the most-tested oils.

Two other reviews point the same way. A 2022 meta-analysis of nine randomized trials by Peng and colleagues in [*Healthcare*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36292438/) found lower bacterial counts in saliva, but no clear difference in plaque or gum scores. A 2020 review in [*Heliyon*](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-oil-pulling-with-coconut-oil-to-improve-dental-hygi/) looked only at coconut oil. It found four trials with 182 people in total, each lasting one to two weeks. Those trials reported lower bacteria and plaque scores, but the authors called the evidence limited, with mixed study quality and a high risk of bias.

None of this shows that oil pulling whitens teeth or stops cavities. Fewer bacteria in a saliva sample is not the same as fewer cavities over years.

## What does the American Dental Association say?

The ADA's consumer site, [MouthHealthy](https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/oil-pulling/), says the association "does not recommend oil pulling as a dental hygiene practice." It notes that no reliable scientific studies show oil pulling reduces cavities. Its advice instead is to brush twice a day for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste and to floss between the teeth once a day.

## Is oil pulling risky?

For most people, the main risk is indirect. Time spent swishing oil is not a substitute for brushing, flossing or dental checkups, and a problem like a toothache or bleeding gums needs a dentist, not a home rinse.

There is also a rare but documented direct risk. Oil that is accidentally breathed into the lungs can cause a condition called exogenous lipoid pneumonia. A [2015 report in *BMC Pulmonary Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26518258/) described two people, a 66-year-old man and a 38-year-old woman, who developed it after months of regular sesame oil pulling. A [2018 report in *Monaldi Archives for Chest Disease*](https://www.monaldi-archives.org/index.php/macd/article/view/922) described two women, both with a history of tongue cancer, whose pneumonia did not clear until they stopped oil pulling and had their lungs washed out. Those authors advised that people at risk of choking or inhaling liquids be told about this risk.

Case reports cannot say how common the problem is. They do show that people with swallowing trouble, a past head or neck cancer, or a tendency to cough while rinsing have extra reason for caution.

## What is a sensible next step?

Anyone curious about oil pulling can bring it up at the next dental visit and ask whether it makes sense for their own teeth and gums. Brushing with fluoride toothpaste and flossing remain the basics either way.
