Ayurveda is a traditional system of medicine from India with a long history, but modern evidence that its remedies work is limited and uneven. Some practices, like diet and daily routine, overlap with ordinary health advice. Some herbal products have been studied only lightly, and a few have documented safety problems.

## Why is Ayurveda in the news?

Ayurveda Day is an annual observance promoted by India's government, and a Telugu Times headline reports that the Consulate General of India in Los Angeles marked Ayurveda Day 2026 with an emphasis on holistic health and sustainable living. Events like this show how far the practice has traveled. It is now a fixture of the global wellness market, sold in shops, spas and supplement aisles far from where it began.

## What does Ayurveda claim?

Ayurveda holds that health depends on balance among three *doshas*, which are energy types called vata, pitta and kapha. Treatment aims to restore that balance through diet, herbs, massage, cleansing routines and lifestyle changes.

The doshas are a traditional framework. They are not anatomical structures, and no modern test measures them. That does not make every Ayurvedic practice useless, but it does mean the underlying theory can't be checked the way a blood pressure reading can.

## What does the research show?

The evidence is sparse and mixed. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) says that although some Ayurvedic approaches have been studied, there is not enough high-quality research to draw firm conclusions about most of them.

There are individual trials. In one randomized trial in people with knee osteoarthritis, an Ayurvedic herbal formulation performed about as well as glucosamine, a common supplement (Chopra et al., 2013, *Rheumatology*). [VERIFY: trial design, sample size, and whether the comparison also included celecoxib.] A single trial of this kind is a starting point. It does not show that the treatment is effective, particularly because glucosamine's own benefits are modest and debated.

Lifestyle elements, such as regular meals, sleep routines and gentle movement, are harder to credit to Ayurveda specifically. Research supports those habits in general, whatever tradition recommends them.

## Is Ayurveda safe?

The main documented risk is contamination. In a 2008 study in *JAMA*, researchers bought Ayurvedic products online and tested them. About one in five contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic (Saper et al., 2008). [VERIFY: 193 products and 20.7% figure.] An earlier *JAMA* study from 2004 found a similar share among products sold in the Boston area.

Some of these metals are added on purpose in traditional preparations. Others get in through poor manufacturing. Either way, long-term exposure to lead, mercury or arsenic can cause serious harm, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration does not approve Ayurvedic products as medicines. In the United States they are generally sold as dietary supplements, which face lighter oversight. [VERIFY: current FDA regulatory language.]

Individual herbs carry their own concerns. Ashwagandha, a popular Ayurvedic herb marketed for stress, has been linked to liver injury in a case series from Iceland and the United States (Björnsson et al., 2020, *Liver International*). Such cases appear to be uncommon, but they are documented. [VERIFY: details of the case series, and any regulatory actions on ashwagandha in Europe.]

Turmeric and its compound curcumin are also widely used. High-dose supplements may interact with blood thinners and other drugs, and rare liver problems have been reported. [VERIFY: source for turmeric supplement liver injury reports and drug interactions.]

## How can someone approach it more carefully?

Tell every clinician about any herbal product being taken, since interactions with prescribed drugs are real. Never stop or swap out prescribed medication or treatment for an herbal remedy. Pregnancy, liver disease, and the use of blood thinners or diabetes drugs all raise the stakes.

Product choice matters too. Independent testing seals, such as those from third-party testing organizations, offer some assurance about contents, though they do not show that a product works. [VERIFY: which labs currently certify supplements.]

## What is a sensible next step?

Before buying any Ayurvedic supplement, write down the product name and ingredients, then bring the list to a pharmacist or doctor and ask whether it could interact with anything else being taken.
