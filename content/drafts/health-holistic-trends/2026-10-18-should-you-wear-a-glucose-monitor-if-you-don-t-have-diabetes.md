For most healthy adults, a continuous glucose monitor is a curiosity, not a necessity. It shows real data, but there is little evidence yet that acting on that data improves long-term health in people without diabetes.

That gap between what the device measures and what the measurement means is the whole story behind the trend.

## What is a continuous glucose monitor?

A continuous glucose monitor, or CGM, is a small sensor worn on the skin, usually on the upper arm. A thin filament sits just under the skin and measures glucose in the fluid between cells. It sends a reading to a phone every few minutes.

CGMs were built for people with diabetes, who use them to manage insulin and avoid dangerous highs and lows. In 2024 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration cleared the first over-the-counter versions: Dexcom's Stelo in March and Abbott's Lingo in June. Both are aimed at adults 18 and older who don't use insulin. [VERIFY: confirm clearance dates and eligibility wording against FDA announcements]

Trade coverage, including a Verywell Health piece aimed at clinicians, now describes patients arriving with CGM data and questions about it.

## What does the wellness trend claim?

The pitch is that food affects everyone differently, and a sensor can show how your body reacts to a given meal. Followers are told to flatten "glucose spikes," the rises in blood sugar after eating, on the theory that smaller swings mean better energy, weight control, and long-term health.

Part of this rests on solid science. The rest is extrapolation.

## Do people without diabetes really have different glucose responses?

Yes. In a 2015 study in *Cell*, Zeevi and colleagues at the Weizmann Institute monitored about 800 people without diabetes for a week. Two people often responded very differently to the same food. Some spiked after bread, others after fruit. The researchers built a model using gut bacteria, lifestyle, and other factors that predicted responses better than counting carbohydrates alone.

A 2018 study in *PLOS Biology* by Hall and colleagues, with 57 mostly healthy participants, found something else. Some people classed as normal by standard tests still showed glucose patterns that rose into the prediabetic range at times. [VERIFY: participant number and framing of "glucotypes" in Hall et al. 2018]

So individual variation is well documented. That is a finding about measurement, not about outcomes.

## Is a "spike" actually harmful?

A rise in glucose after eating is normal. The body is supposed to do it, then bring levels back down. A 2019 multicenter study in *Diabetes Technology & Therapeutics* (Shah and colleagues) wore CGMs on healthy adults and described what typical readings look like, including brief rises above 140 mg/dL after meals. [VERIFY: Shah et al. 2019 sample size and the proportion of time spent above 140 mg/dL]

Whether smaller spikes lead to better health in people with normal metabolism has not been established. Research suggests that sustained high glucose matters for people with diabetes. No large trial has shown that a healthy person who tunes their meals to a CGM ends up healthier years later. [VERIFY: check for any recent randomized trials of CGM use in people without diabetes]

## Can a CGM help anyone without diabetes?

It may help some people notice patterns. Seeing a reading climb after a sugary drink, or stay steady after a meal with protein and fiber, can make habits feel concrete. Walking after meals is often cited as a way to blunt a rise, and that is a low-risk habit regardless.

The honest limit is that these are short-term behavior nudges. They are not proof of a health benefit.

## What are the downsides?

There are several, and they are practical.

- **Accuracy.** CGMs measure glucose in tissue fluid, not blood. Readings lag behind blood by several minutes and are less reliable at the extremes and during the first day of wear.
- **Skin reactions.** Adhesive irritation and allergic rashes are reported with wearable sensors.
- **Interference.** Some substances can skew certain sensors. Acetaminophen and high-dose vitamin C have affected some models. Check the label for your specific device. [VERIFY: current interference warnings for Stelo and Lingo]
- **Anxiety.** Constant numbers can push some people toward rigid eating. Anyone with a history of disordered eating should talk to a clinician before trying one.
- **Cost.** Sensors are sold in small batches and the expense adds up. [VERIFY: current retail prices]

A CGM also cannot diagnose diabetes. Fasting glucose, A1C, or an oral glucose tolerance test, ordered by a clinician, do that. If you have symptoms such as unusual thirst, frequent urination, or unexplained fatigue, those tests are the route, not a consumer sensor.

## What's a sensible next step?

If the curiosity is strong, bring it to a clinician first. Ask whether your family history or symptoms warrant a standard blood test, and whether a short CGM trial would add anything useful in your case. That conversation costs little and gives the data context.
