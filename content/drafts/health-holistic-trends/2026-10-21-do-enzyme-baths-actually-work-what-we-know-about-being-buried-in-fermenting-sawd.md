Enzyme baths are warm, and that part is real. Whether the enzymes do anything for your body is another matter: no solid clinical evidence shows they do. The experience is better understood as a form of heat therapy with a fermentation twist.

## What is an enzyme bath?

An enzyme bath is a spa treatment in which a person lies in a tub of plant material, usually cedar sawdust and rice bran, and is covered up to the neck. The mixture is fermented, and the microbial activity gives off heat. Versions of the treatment are offered in Japan, where it is sometimes called *kōso-yoku*, and The Korea Herald has reported on it as a growing wellness trend in Korea. [VERIFY: typical temperature reported for the bath, often cited as roughly 50 to 70°C, and the Korea Herald's specific claims about venues and prices.]

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction. In fermentation, microbes and enzymes break down the plant material, and that breakdown releases heat. The bath is warm because of this process.

## What do enzyme baths claim to do?

Spas and promoters tend to describe the baths as detoxifying, circulation-boosting and good for skin and stress. Some also say the enzymes themselves are absorbed through the skin and act on the body. These are marketing claims, and they vary by venue.

## What does the evidence show?

For the enzyme-specific claims, very little. The term "detox" has no clear meaning here. The liver and kidneys handle waste removal, and no study has shown that sweating in a fermenting bath adds to that. Enzymes are large protein molecules, and intact skin is built to keep large molecules out. That makes meaningful absorption unlikely, though no study of enzyme baths has tested it directly. [VERIFY: confirm no peer-reviewed clinical trials of enzyme baths exist; search PubMed for "enzyme bath" and "kōso-yoku."]

The heat is a different story. Passive heat exposure has been studied, mostly in saunas. A 2015 study in *JAMA Internal Medicine* by Laukkanen and colleagues followed more than 2,000 Finnish men and found that more frequent sauna use was associated with lower rates of fatal cardiovascular events. The study was observational, which means it can show a link but not prove that saunas caused the difference. It also looked at dry saunas, not buried-in-sawdust baths.

Related evidence comes from balneotherapy, the medical term for bathing in mineral water or mud. Cochrane reviews have looked at it for osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. They found some short-term symptom relief in small trials, with weak study quality. [VERIFY: Verhagen et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2007 (osteoarthritis) and 2015 (rheumatoid arthritis); confirm conclusions and wording before publishing.] Even if that holds up, it speaks to warm-water or mud soaking in general, not to enzyme baths.

## Why does it feel good, then?

Heat relaxes muscles, raises skin temperature and heart rate, and for many people feels calming. Lying still in a quiet room for 10 to 20 minutes also has its own appeal. Those effects are plausible and match what people report. They don't need enzymes to explain them, and no one has shown that the fermentation adds anything beyond warmth.

## Are there risks?

Yes, and they come mostly from the heat. Prolonged hot exposure can cause dehydration, dizziness, fainting and burns, and buried bodies can't easily cool off or move. People with heart conditions, low blood pressure, pregnancy, or reduced sensation in the skin (as can occur with diabetes) are generally told to be cautious with saunas and hot baths. Some medications affect blood pressure or heat tolerance, so check with a prescriber if you take any.

Two other points. Wood dust and rice bran can trigger reactions in people with allergies or sensitive skin or airways. And because the material is a living fermenting mix, hygiene practices and how often it is replaced matter. ## What's the fair summary?

The trend rests on a pleasant heat experience wrapped in enzyme language that the science hasn't backed up. For a healthy adult, a short supervised session is probably a lot like a sauna. For anyone with a medical condition, it carries real risks. It is not a substitute for any prescribed treatment.

## A next step

If you're thinking of booking a session, ask the venue for the bath temperature and recommended session length, and run both past your doctor first.
