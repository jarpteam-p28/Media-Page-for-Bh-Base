The cognitive shuffle is a bedtime mental exercise: instead of lying awake with the same worries, a person pictures a string of random, unrelated things until sleep arrives. It is free and has no known downside, and some early studies are encouraging. But the research is thin, has been done mostly by the technique's creator and his colleagues, and has not shown that it beats other approaches.

## Why is the cognitive shuffle suddenly everywhere?

The technique is circulating in wellness newsletters again. An October 2026 wellness roundup on Substack called it one of the quirkiest sleep strategies its author had tried and described the research as "still preliminary" ([WELL-BENT](https://wellbent.substack.com/p/october-wellness-edit-2026)). It drew wider attention in April 2025 through a CNN feature, republished by New Zealand's RNZ ([RNZ/CNN](https://www.rnz.co.nz/life/wellbeing/cognitive-shuffling-a-mental-trick-to-help-you-quiet-racing-thoughts-and-fall-asleep)).

## Who came up with it?

The technique comes from Luc Beaudoin, a cognitive scientist and adjunct professor of education at Simon Fraser University in British Columbia. According to CNN, he began working on it as an undergraduate nearly 40 years ago, when he struggled to sleep on Sunday nights, and the name "cognitive shuffling" was in use by 2009 ([RNZ/CNN](https://www.rnz.co.nz/life/wellbeing/cognitive-shuffling-a-mental-trick-to-help-you-quiet-racing-thoughts-and-fall-asleep)). In research papers it is called serial diverse imagining.

Beaudoin has a commercial stake. A disclosure on his 2016 conference poster says he is a director and shareholder of CogSci Apps Corp., which develops the mySleepButton and SomnoTest apps, and owner of CogZest, which provides training in insomnia and cognitive productivity ([Beaudoin et al., SLEEP 2016 handout](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/16196/Handout-Beaudoin%2C%20Digdon%20et%20al%20SLEEP-2016%20SDI%20Poster.pdf)).

## How is it supposed to work?

One common version, as described in the CNN piece: choose a random, emotionally neutral word. For each letter, think of as many words as possible that start with it, and picture each one for five to eight seconds. The words do not need to connect.

The idea rests on Beaudoin's "somnolent mentation theory." As people drift off, their thinking normally becomes loose, image-heavy and incoherent. The theory proposes that this jumbled thinking is not just a side effect of falling asleep but actually helps it along, while problem-solving and sense-making keep the brain alert ([Digdon and Beaudoin, CogSci 2015 abstract](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/25896/galley/15520/download)). The shuffle is meant to imitate that drifting state on purpose and crowd out worry at the same time. This is a hypothesis, not an established mechanism.

## What does the research actually show?

The main study usually cited was presented as a poster at SLEEP 2016, a joint meeting of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society. It involved 154 university students, 137 of them female, with an average age of about 20, who reported a racing mind at bedtime. They were randomly assigned to one of three groups: the shuffle, delivered by an app that read out a random word every eight seconds; structured problem-solving, which means setting aside 15 minutes earlier in the day to write down worries and next steps; or both ([Beaudoin et al., SLEEP 2016 poster](https://summit.sfu.ca/_flysystem/fedora/sfu_migrate/16196/2016-06-06%201_23%20PM%20SLEEP%202016-06%20Beaudoin%2C%20Digdon%20et%20al%20Denver.graffle.pdf)).

After about a month, all three groups reported less pre-sleep arousal, less effort trying to sleep and better sleep quality than at the start. The groups did not differ significantly from each other. The authors themselves noted that this comparison could not be interpreted, because the study lacked the statistical power to detect a difference. Students who tried both methods were more likely to rate the shuffle as more helpful and easier to use.

That result has clear limits:

- There was no group that received nothing new, so the improvement cannot be credited to either technique.
- The outcomes were self-reported questionnaires, not measured sleep.
- The participants were mostly young women at one university.
- The researchers include the technique's creator.
- Searches for this article did not turn up a full peer-reviewed journal paper reporting the trial.

A broader 2020 systematic review in *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, which Beaudoin co-authored, looked at studies of mental strategies such as imagery and distraction at bedtime. Its abstract says their effects on falling asleep or insomnia were "either beneficial, negligible, or detrimental," and it calls for more research on how thinking affects sleep onset ([Lemyre et al., 2020](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1353766/pre-sleep-cognitive-activity-in-adults-a-systemati)). In a 2025 interview, Beaudoin said a fourth study comparing the shuffle with other techniques was planned ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-innovator-next-door/202504/a-creatively-random-idea-for-falling-asleep)).

## Who probably won't be helped by it?

The shuffle is aimed at a busy mind at bedtime. Beaudoin told CNN that someone who drinks a lot of coffee before bed should not expect a mental technique to help them sleep. Experts in the same article said it does not replace good sleep habits, does not fix a sleep disorder and is not a substitute for cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, known as CBT-I. They also suggested getting out of bed after about 20 minutes of trying, doing something calm elsewhere, and returning later ([RNZ/CNN](https://www.rnz.co.nz/life/wellbeing/cognitive-shuffling-a-mental-trick-to-help-you-quiet-racing-thoughts-and-fall-asleep)). The studies described here did not report on side effects.

Ongoing sleep trouble is a different matter. The American College of Physicians defines chronic insomnia as symptoms on at least three nights a week for at least three months that cause real distress or trouble functioning. Its 2016 guideline, published in *Annals of Internal Medicine*, says CBT-I should be the first-line treatment for adults with chronic insomnia ([ACP](https://www.acponline.org/acp-newsroom/acp-recommends-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-as-initial-treatment-for-chronic-insomnia)). CBT-I combines changing unhelpful thoughts about sleep with behavioral steps such as limiting time in bed.

## What is a sensible next step?

For an occasional night of racing thoughts, the shuffle costs nothing to try for a few nights. If poor sleep has become a regular pattern, book an appointment with a doctor and ask whether CBT-I is a good fit.
