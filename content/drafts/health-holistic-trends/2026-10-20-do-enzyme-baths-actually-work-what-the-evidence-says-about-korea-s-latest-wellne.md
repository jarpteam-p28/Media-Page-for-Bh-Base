An enzyme bath is a heat treatment, and heat is the part with real evidence behind it. The claims that the "enzymes" detoxify the body or deliver special benefits through the skin have little direct research to support them.

## What is an enzyme bath?

An enzyme bath is a tub filled with plant material, usually cedar sawdust and rice bran, that is left to ferment. Microbes break the material down and give off heat as they do. Bathers lie down and are covered in the warm mixture, often for around 15 to 20 minutes [VERIFY: typical session length and operating temperature at Korean venues; the Korea Herald piece should be checked for specifics].

The practice is better documented in Japan, where it is sometimes called *koso yoku*. A recent Korea Herald report describes it as a growing wellness trend in Korea.

## What do enzyme baths claim to do?

Operators and fans typically point to a short list of benefits: better circulation, sweating out toxins, smoother skin, and a faster metabolism. The word "enzyme" carries much of the marketing weight.

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction. Fermentation involves many of them. But whether enzymes in the bath material do anything useful once they touch intact skin is a separate question, and no good human trial that tests it is known to this writer [VERIFY: search PubMed for controlled studies of "enzyme bath" or "cedar bran bath"].

## Does the heat do anything?

Probably something, though not specifically from fermentation. Warmth relaxes muscles and widens blood vessels near the skin. Many people find it calming, and that experience is genuine.

The closest large body of research is on saunas. A 2015 study in *JAMA Internal Medicine* followed 2,315 middle-aged men in eastern Finland. It found that men who used saunas more often had lower rates of cardiovascular death. The study was observational, meaning it could not prove that saunas caused the difference. Men who sauna often may differ in other ways. It also studied dry saunas, not fermenting-bran baths.

So the evidence for heat is suggestive. The evidence for enzyme baths specifically is thin to absent.

## Can you sweat out toxins?

Sweat does contain trace amounts of some substances. A 2011 review-style study by Genuis and colleagues in *Archives of Environmental Contamination and Toxicology* compared sweat, blood, and urine for various metals and chemicals, and found some were detectable in sweat. That finding is often quoted to support detox claims.

It does not show that sweating meaningfully lowers the amount of these substances in the body, or that it improves health. The liver and kidneys do the heavy lifting of clearing waste. Most of what sweat is made of is water and salt.

## What are the risks?

Any prolonged heat exposure carries them. Possible problems include dehydration, dizziness, a drop in blood pressure, fainting, and overheating. Pregnant people, and those with heart conditions, low blood pressure, or heat sensitivity should ask a clinician first. Some medications also change how the body handles heat, including diuretics, blood pressure drugs, and some antihistamines and antidepressants [VERIFY: confirm drug classes against a clinical source such as the CDC or NHS heat guidance].

Skin is a separate concern. Bathing in organic material that is hot and damp can irritate sensitive skin or aggravate eczema. Hygiene depends on how often the material is replaced, which varies by venue and is hard for a customer to check. Anyone with open wounds or a skin condition should be cautious.

## Is it worth trying?

As a warm, quiet hour, it may well be. As a way to detoxify or treat a condition, nothing in the available research supports it. Treat it like a spa session rather than a health intervention, drink water, and leave if you feel lightheaded.

For anyone weighing it up, one step is worth taking: ask the venue how often the bath material is changed and what the temperature is, before you book.
